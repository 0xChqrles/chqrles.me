Among the various ways I’ve found to waste time in front of a screen, there are daily games. One of them is called **Semantle**.

Every day, a word is picked at random and you have to guess it with a semantic hot-and-cold game. At first you type random words, then you eventually get lucky and land on a word closer than the others. Then you follow the trail, testing similar words until you find the word of the day. The game is fun but repetitive, and finding the right word sometimes takes hundreds of tries for a reward that doesn’t always live up to the effort. “Bravo, you found the word cloakroom in 245 tries, see you tomorrow.” I was supposed to be diverted and forget my condition as a mortal. In the end, Semantle mostly amplified my perception of wasted time and of the emptiness of my existence. Thanks, Semantle.

Like the electron that is everywhere at once as long as you don’t measure it, a word on its own carries all its meanings as long as you don’t use it. The semantic value of a word on its own isn’t enough to understand what it expresses, and that may be what Semantle is missing. What’s left is to work out how to measure the semantic distance between words depending on their context.
## Embedding

Let’s first look at how Semantle calculates the distance between words. A distance is a numerical value, so we have to find a way to express the meaning of words in numerical form too. Basically, expressing complex information as numbers has a name, it’s called an **embedding**. We could, for example, give words coordinates, which would then make it easy to measure how close two words are.

The idea looks good, but the more words we add to our plane, the harder it gets to spread them out intelligently, and two dimensions won’t be enough for long. So we’ll go from 2 to, let’s say, 300 dimensions. That’s much harder to draw, but far more practical for filing away hundreds of thousands of words. The real problem finally takes shape. How do we choose the 300 coordinates of each word?

### Skip-gram / word2vec

We start by assigning a random vector to each word. At this stage, our coordinates mean absolutely nothing, and to give them meaning we’ll ask the network to do something fairly simple: given a word, predict the ones likely to appear around it. We give it `cat` and it assigns a probability to every word it knows. Since everything is still more or less random, its first answers look like this:

Now we need to find a way to explain to it that it’s talking nonsense. For that, we build a large reference corpus from carefully selected texts: books, Wikipedia pages, academic articles, for example.
Now let’s take an ambitious sentence from our corpus:

```text /cat/
The cat eats a mouse
```

We pick `cat` and look at what appears around it: `the`, `eats`, `a`. That’s what the network is supposed to predict. If it considers `engine` far more likely than `eats`, it has obviously made a mistake. What’s left is to quantify by how much, so we sum up all its errors in a score we call the **loss**: the higher it is, the worse its predictions.

We now know how to measure the problem, but not yet how to fix it. The network contains a huge number of parameters, among them the coordinates of our vectors, and we need to know which ones to move and in which direction. For each one, we calculate the effect a tiny change would have on the loss. Basically, for each vector we answer the question:

> If I change this coordinate very slightly, does the *loss* go up or down, and by how much?

All these variations make up the **gradient**, a vector that points in the direction in which the loss would increase the most, so we do something very sophisticated: we go the other way.

Each of these variations makes up a **gradient**. A vector that points in the direction in which the loss would increase the most, so we do something very sophisticated: we go the other way. We slightly change the parameters, make a new prediction, recalculate the error, then start over. That’s **gradient descent**.

After just a few billion repetitions, the loss starts to come down in earnest. But what interests us most is what all these corrections did to our vectors. Let’s imagine the network regularly comes across this kind of sentence:

```text /cat/ /dog/
The cat sleeps on the couch
The dog sleeps on the rug

I give my cat something to eat
I give my dog something to eat

The cat runs in the garden
The dog runs in the garden
```

`cat` and `dog` obviously live fairly similar lives. They `sleep`, `eat`, `run`, so they appear surrounded by comparable words. To succeed at predicting their contexts, the network ends up giving them representations that look alike. Nobody explained to it that a cat and a dog were two fairly close domestic animals. In fact, it still doesn’t know what a cat is. It simply noticed that `cat` often hangs out with the same words as `dog`, and that’s enough.

As it keeps correcting its predictions, the completely random vectors we started with end up organizing themselves. Words used in similar contexts occupy similar regions of our 300-dimensional space. At the end of training, we take these vectors and use them directly as the semantic coordinates of our words: we have just created an **embedding**.

## One word, a single vector

Now that we have the Semantle recipe, to make a version with context and offer a Pascalian diversion worthy of the name, we take a lovely sentence from a book or a song, we hide a few words, and the player has to find them. And there you go, we’ve created Semantle with context.

Except our embedding has a birth defect: it’s **static**. It assigns a single vector to each word, whatever the sentence. Now, a word doesn’t have just one meaning. During training, `bank` hung out as often with rivers as with Nancy Pelosi, and its vector ended up somewhere in between, in a place that is neither quite the shore nor quite an offshore account.

As long as we’re guessing a word without context, this fuzziness isn’t really a problem, since the word of the day itself has no precise meaning. But let’s take this sentence:

```text /banks/
Down beside where the waters flow, down by the banks of the Ohio
```

No robbers here. Yet here are the neighbors of `banks` according to the embedding:

Half the list by the river, the other half in the biography of Bonnie and Clyde. But let’s take another, more subtle example:

The embedding understood “to treat a subject.” The sentence, though, is about treating someone. A player who types tend or spare has perfectly understood the sentence, yet the game tells them they’re far off. From this injustice, frustration is born, and frustration, that leads to war. So giving the player a sentence isn’t enough. The embedding, too, has to read the sentence.

## First brilliant idea: reading ChatGPT’s mind

Despite the immensity of the universe, as far as we know there are only two kinds of beings capable of understanding human language. Humans themselves and, very recently, GPT and its fellow LLMs. The second also has the advantage of handling 300-dimensional vectors better, so we’ll turn to it.

### Transformers

Before talking about a full LLM, let’s start with the building block it’s mostly made of, the **Transformer**. We’ve seen how to represent words as vectors. A Transformer receives several of these vectors and transforms them so that each one can take in information coming from the other words in the sentence. All it takes then is to pass a sentence through several layers of Transformers to get vectors that no longer represent just the words themselves, but the words in their context. On paper, Transformers look like the ideal tool to solve our problem.

The mechanism that lets words pick up information from one another is called **attention**. For each word, the Transformer builds three new vectors from its current representation:

```
Q (query): what I’m looking for
K (key):   in which cases I can be relevant
V (value): the information I pass on if I’m listened to
```

Let’s take the following sentence and focus on the word `bank`:

```text /bank/
The duck swims to the bank
```

The Transformer receives its vector and computes its **Q (query)**. We can imagine it expresses something like:

> Which pieces of information in the context are important to understand my role here?

The other words, for their part, have a **K (key)**, and to determine which ones are interesting, the Transformer compares the query of `bank` with the different keys using a **dot product**. The dot product depends on both the length of the two vectors and the angle between them. At equal length, the more they point in the same direction, the bigger the result; perpendicular, we get zero, and in opposite directions, it goes negative. We can therefore use it as a compatibility score between a **Q (query)** and a **K (key)**, and these scores are then converted to percentages that add up to 100% (thanks to a softmax, for the experts).

`bank` has thus just taken in a lot of information from `duck`, without the model having been given a handwritten rule saying:

> If the subject is a duck, then bank probably means the side of a river.

The model simply learned that, in this kind of context, the information contained in `duck` is very useful for correctly representing `bank`. The matrices that produce Q, K and V are themselves parameters of the network. At the start of training, they produce essentially nonsense, then they are gradually adjusted by **gradient descent**, like our **embeddings**.

Once attention is done, each word has a new representation, enriched by what it picked up from its context. It then goes through a more standard neural network, then becomes the input of the next Transformer layer. And we start over a few dozen times. At each layer, new Q, K and V are computed from the representations produced by the previous one. A first layer may learn a relatively simple relationship between `duck` and `bank`, then the following ones work from this already integrated information and progressively build richer representations.

To train a model like GPT, we give it a task fairly close to the one we used for embeddings: predicting what comes next in a text. We show it, for example, `The duck`, and it has to predict `swims`, then `The duck swims`, and it has to predict `to`, and so on over billions of pieces of text. At each prediction, we calculate the **loss**, then the **gradients**, and we slightly change all the parameters. After predicting what comes next in billions of sentences, the model gradually learns which words should listen to each other and which information should flow between them. That’s how a huge stack of Transformer layers, trained with the rather basic goal of guessing the next word, ends up building something that looks dangerously like a nuanced understanding of context.

### Opening the hood

At each layer of the network, each word therefore has a vector that has listened to its context. This intermediate vector has a name, the **hidden state**. It’s exactly what Semantle was missing: an embedding that has read the sentence and can tell the `bank` a duck swims to from the `bank` that finances settlements in the West Bank. In normal use of an LLM, we don’t look directly at these vectors, we let the model go all the way and read the text it produces. But nothing forbids us from stopping it along the way and retrieving the vector produced by the layer we’re interested in.

Nothing, except OpenAI. ChatGPT doesn’t let us open its hood, so we need a model whose parameters we can download and that we can run ourselves. Fortunately, market communism works in mysterious ways, and Alibaba hands out the weights of its **Qwen** model for free.

### The plan

Let’s go back to our sentence:

```text /bank/
The duck swims to the bank
```

1. We take the 10,000 words closest to `bank` according to our standard Semantle-style embedding.

<Words lists={[{ words: ['shore', 'loan', 'creek', 'vault', 'slope', 'deposit', 'riverside', 'robbery', 'heist', 'teller'] }]} />

2. We write 10,000 versions of our sentence, replacing `bank` with each of these words.

```text /shore/ /loan/ /creek/
The duck swims to the shore
The duck swims to the loan
The duck swims to the creek
...
```

3. We show these 10,000 sentences to the LLM, each time we retrieve the **hidden state** of the tested word, and we calculate its distance from that of `bank` in the original sentence.

4. We sort the list again, but by this contextual distance.

<Words lists={[{ words: ['shore', 'riverside', 'embankment', 'slope', 'waterside'] }]} />

We’ve got our solution. We keep Semantle to quickly find candidates, then we use an LLM to eliminate the ones with the wrong meaning. We no longer compare `bank` and `loan` in a vacuum, we first let them read their respective sentences, then we compare what they’ve become. In theory, the idea is perfect.

And yet the article isn’t even halfway through. The theory had left out three important details.

### Problem 1: causal mask

First detail glossed over. During attention, a word can only listen to what comes before it. This is called the **causal mask**, and it makes sense: the model is trained to guess what comes next, so we’re not going to show it. Let’s take a new sentence:

```text /bank/ /robbed/
The bank was robbed by a pigeon
```

<Arcs
  caption="When the model computes `bank`, `robbed` hasn’t arrived yet."
  tokens={['The', 'bank', 'was', 'robbed', 'by', 'a', 'pigeon']}
  from={1}
  to={[{ token: 0 }]}
  mask="causal"
/>

When the model computes the **hidden state** of `bank`, it has heard `The`, but it has never heard of `robbed`. The most useful part of the sentence is invisible to it, and the model has no idea a pigeon is about to rob the place. For a word to have read the whole sentence, we have to place it after the sentence:

```text /<TARGET>/ /bank/
Context:
The <TARGET> was robbed by a pigeon

Lexeme:
bank
```

This time, `bank` has read everything. Unfortunately, nothing really pushes it to make the connection with the sentence above, and its vector falls back on a fairly general meaning, close to that of the static embedding:

<Words lists={[{ words: ['loan', 'shore', 'vault', 'creek', 'deposit', 'riverside', 'teller'] }]} />

### Problem 2: word ≠ token

From the start, I’ve been acting as if an LLM read words. That’s wrong. It reads **tokens**, pieces of text chosen to be reusable. A common word can fit in a single token, a rarer word can be split into several, and it then has one **hidden state** per token. We have to choose which one to use, and since each token can only read what comes before it, the last one is a fairly natural candidate: it has at least seen all the previous pieces of the same word.

In the case of Qwen 3, for example, `bedbug` is split into `bed-` and `-bug`. Here is what we then find among the nearest neighbors of its last token, `-bug`:

<Words
  sentence="we can tell the bedbug from the man less and less"
  lists={[{ words: ['rug', 'mug', 'thug', 'slug', 'drug', 'shrug'] }]}
  marks={['ug']}
/>

Not a single insect, whereas the static embedding offered `flea`, `cockroach` and `tick`. We just turned Semantle into a rhyming dictionary.

*Note that the tick isn’t an insect but an arachnid.*

### Problem 3: LLMs predict tokens, not distances

Third detail, and the deepest. Our Semantle-style embedding was trained so that words used in similar contexts get close vectors. An LLM, for its part, is trained with another objective: predicting the next token. Nothing guarantees, then, that the raw geometry of its hidden states makes a good semantic distance. These vectors are above all intermediate representations, useful to the model to continue its calculation.

First symptom, the vectors look far too much alike. With our static embedding, we measured the closeness between two words with **cosine similarity**, a dot product between two vectors previously scaled to a length of 1. That leaves only the angle: two vectors pointing in the same direction are considered very similar, regardless of their length.

In an ideal embedding, the vectors would be fairly well spread out in all directions. But the hidden states of an LLM tend to pile up in the same region of space. As a result, even two words that have nothing to do with each other already share a large part of their direction. In my tests, the similarities are almost always between 0.45 and 0.88, on a scale where 1 means two vectors point in exactly the same direction and 0 that they’re perpendicular. This phenomenon has a name, **anisotropy**.

We can try to remove part of it. If all the vectors tend to point to the same place, we calculate their mean vector, then subtract it from each of them. Basically, we take away from everyone what they had in common before measuring their differences again. That spreads the candidates out a bit better and makes their similarities more discriminating. But not enough.

Second symptom, in the last layers, the model no longer thinks only about the meaning of the word. Mostly, it prepares what would make sense at that point in the sentence:

<Words
  sentence="until their retirement"
  lists={[{ words: ['graduation', 'wedding', 'strike'] }]}

Events that fit very well after “until their,” but have little to do with `retirement`. The model largely measures how well the candidate works with what surrounds it, whereas we want to measure how well its **meaning** matches that of the hidden word. We wanted a semantic distance, we built a fill-in-the-blank exercise.

The project seemed doomed to fail, but poking around in the guts of an LLM was still more fun than going back to Semantle with my tail between my legs to guess the word “wiener” in 328 tries. So I kept looking and I ended up finding (sort of).

### Turning the problem against itself

Since the model is obsessed with what comes next in the sentence, we might as well turn the problem against it and make sure that what comes next is precisely the answer we’re looking for:

```text /:/2
Context:
A gold digger and a broke guy, that doesn’t go together

In this context, a word close to “broke”:
```

This time, we no longer retrieve the vector of `broke` but that of the colon, at the very end of the prompt. That token has read everything: the sentence, the word we’re interested in and the instruction. And since its role is to prepare what comes right after, we can imagine that at that moment the model is asking itself a question fairly close to ours:

> I’m going to have to write a word that means roughly the same thing as `broke` in this sentence.

So we’re no longer measuring the representation of the word itself, with its spelling and its possible split into several tokens, but the state of the model just before it offers an answer. This incidentally solves our problem with words split like `bedbug`: it doesn’t matter whether they take up one or five tokens, by the time it reaches the colon the model has read them all. And by looking layer by layer, on a model that has 36 of them, we can even see the answer gradually sharpen:

### It’s better but it’s not quite there yet

Except this solution generalizes rather poorly. Spelling lookalikes keep rising in the rankings, `angle` for example ends up as the 4th neighbor of `angel`, and the top of the lists is sometimes downright noisy. In `they were perfect nerves`, `nerves` ends up surrounded by `organs`, `thymus` and `viscères`.

Above all, I have no way of really talking to the model. A vector gives me a distance, but it doesn’t let me explain to it what shouldn’t count. If a word is rewarded simply because it rhymes with the answer, I can’t write “stop with the rhymes” anywhere. I can only change the prompt and pray. In fact I tried several phrasings, they all produced different rankings, but none really stood out for its relevance.

To avoid unfairly blaming LLMs on the basis of a single model, I repeated the experiment across several generations and architectures:

### Qwen 3

I first tested **Qwen 3**, which uses the standard Transformer architecture we just described, in several sizes from 0.6 to 14 billion parameters, without quantization. Increasing the size didn’t always improve the results, and the same underlying flaws kept coming back.

### Qwen 3.5

**Qwen 3.5** uses a more recent architecture: in three layers out of four, standard attention is replaced by a cheaper linear memory mechanism (Gated DeltaNet, for the experts again). It made no difference to our problem. `bedbug` keeps rhyming, and on six ambiguous words this model even proved less effective than its previous generation. A juicy detail: for the `banks` of the Ohio, the 4-billion-parameter version understood the river, and the 9-billion one, the vault. By making the model bigger, I had lost relevance. My approach was clearly not the right one.

### Qwen 3 – Embedding

As it happens, there are LLMs retrained to produce good vectors. This time, they are explicitly taught that semantically close texts must produce close representations, and that unrelated texts must move apart. These are **embedding models**, and it’s exactly the property our cobbled-together **hidden states** were missing. Qwen thus offers **Qwen3-Embedding**, a family built from **Qwen 3** and then specially trained to produce representations intended for search and semantic comparison. I tested it in several ways. The results were at the same mediocre level as my **hidden states**.

### Qwen 3 – Reranker

Qwen also distributes **Qwen3-Reranker**, perhaps the most promising candidate on paper. When you do a Google search, the engine first gathers thousands of potentially relevant pages, then reranks them to put the best ones first. This second sort is the job of a reranker, a model that rereads the search alongside each page and judges whether it answers it. That looks like our problem, phrased differently. The sentence, with its hidden word, is our Google search, and the Semantle embedding that provides us with 10,000 potentially relevant words, those are the results to rerank. This time, no distance to calculate and no Transformer guts to poke around in, we simply ask the reranker to do its job.

It reads `broke` and `bank` the way their sentences mean them, but a reranker is first designed to judge how relevant a result is to a search. So it brings up words that go well with the search, not necessarily those that mean the same thing, and `unemployed` comes second for `broke`. Which makes sense, but it’s not exactly what we’re trying to get here.

In my tests, it also had an unfortunate tendency to take the instruction for part of the search. Even when I explicitly told it that `stifle` should not be considered close to `emotions`, it kept ranking it among the very first. A bit as if I had typed “recipe without peanuts” into Google and the search engine had focused on `recipe` and `peanut`.

So that’s it? I’m nothing but a two-bit tinkerer unable to improve the horrible Semantle, and my fate is to keep being tortured daily by ever more absurd words?

## The solution: stop measuring, ask the question

That’s when I came across **Jev**, a decision model released in September 2026 by TypeSafe. So what is this famous model made of, the one that succeeded where LLMs failed? We don’t know exactly, TypeSafe talks about a new architecture without publishing the details. But we can still get a fairly good idea of the principle. Take a model capable of understanding text, then replace its calling as a writer with that of a judge. A standard LLM ends its calculation by trying to answer this question:

> Which token should I write next?

A decision model can use a representation of the same kind, but end with a small specialized layer, a **classifier**, which answers this instead:

> Among these answers, which one seems correct, and with what probability?

Simplifying enormously, it looks something like this:

/>

The bulk of the work stays the same: reading language, understanding the relationships between words and building a representation of the context. Only at the end do we ask it for something else, and that’s precisely what we need. We spent all this time retrieving the intermediate vectors of a model trained to write, hoping their geometry would nevertheless meet our goal. This time, we can simply ask the question:

> In this sentence, how close is this word to that one?

Let’s look at the new plan.

### A rating for each candidate

Semantle keeps its role as the beater. It provides the 10,000 neighbors of the hidden word, without worrying about context, and each one then receives a rating from Jev:

```
0: no relation in meaning with the secret word as used in the sentence
1: distant relation, same very general domain
2: related, same lexical field or neighboring idea
3: very close, near-synonym
4: same meaning, direct synonym in this context
```

The instruction is written in plain language: only meaning counts, not grammar, not spelling, not whether the candidate can replace the word in the sentence. Let’s take `nerves` again:

<Words
  lists={[
    { label: 'Qwen', words: ['organs', 'thymus', 'minds', 'medical', 'marrows', 'viscères'] },
    { label: 'Jev', words: ['nervousness', 'excitability', 'nervous', 'irritation', 'irritable'] },
  ]}
/>

Jev understood we were talking about temperament and not neurology. As for rhymes and spelling neighbors, they’re gone for good. At first, though, Jev also made mistakes:

<Words
  sentence="the strength to stifle my emotions"
  lists={[{ label: 'Word 28', words: ['stifle'] }]}
  marks={['stifle']}
/>

`stifle` isn’t a word close to `emotions`, it’s a word from the sentence. So I added a line to the instruction:

> A candidate that appears in the sentence, or that describes what the sentence does with the secret word, is not any closer because of that.

`stifle` went from 28th to 1,182nd place. Poking around in the **hidden states**, I could observe that a result was bad, but not explain to the vector why. Here, if Jev gets it wrong for a reason I can put into words, I can simply tell it.

Not everything can be fixed with words, though. I had also written that a candidate that isn’t English deserved the lowest rating, and Jev royally ignored it. `retraite` stayed the first neighbor of `retirement`. I ended up fixing this with code, by demoting words three times more frequent in a French corpus than in an English corpus.

### A tournament to break the tie among the winners

Jev isn’t perfectly stable either. If I ask it several times to rate the same candidate, its answer can vary by about 0.1 point. At the bottom of the ranking, that doesn’t change much. The 7,400th word can become the 7,500th, it won’t shake up the game. At the top of the ranking, it’s different. That’s where the order matters most, and if the words ranked 50th and 150th have almost identical ratings, such a variation is enough to make them interchangeable. So Jev knows fairly well which words deserve to enter the top 200, but much less well in what order to rank them.

So we’re going to organize a tournament. The 200 best candidates all face each other, two by two, that is 19,900 duels, with a single question:

> Which of these two words is closer to the secret word in this sentence?

For each duel, Jev gives both candidates a probability of winning, and a word’s final score is simply its average probability of winning across all its matchups. A standard sorting algorithm would need far fewer comparisons. But a sort assumes we can trust the function that compares two elements. If `A > B` and `B > C`, we’d reasonably like to be able to conclude that `A > C`. Jev, on the other hand, may very well answer that `A < C`, it is neither perfectly deterministic nor perfectly transitive. In a standard sort, one bad comparison could send a word to the wrong place and influence everything that follows. In a full tournament, each candidate faces the other 199, and if it makes a strange decision, it gets diluted among 198 other matchups.

And it really works. For the secret word `coldly`, `impassivity` goes from 8th to 2nd place. For `beauty`, `fascination` climbs from 152nd to 13th.

Finally, beyond the 300th rank or so, I simply keep Semantle’s initial order. Nobody will notice, but now you know.

### Why does it work?

We still have to do justice to LLMs. Nothing I ask of Jev seems out of their reach. I could take a good model, show it the sentence and two candidates, then ask it which one is semantically closer to the secret word. It would probably answer very well. The problem is that I’d have to ask it the question tens of thousands of times, and an idea that’s perfectly reasonable on paper becomes much less appealing when each little decision costs a few seconds and a request to a big model.

Jev can’t do anything an LLM would be unable to do, but it can make a small semantic decision extremely fast and for almost nothing. For one secret word, my system has it process about 3.1 million tokens, and by caching the fixed part of the instruction, the whole thing costs me about $0.13. So I can afford a strategy of remarkable brutality: have 10,000 candidates judged one by one, keep the best 200, then organize 19,900 additional duels just to rank them better. With a slower or more expensive model, I’d have spent my time looking for a clever algorithm to reduce the number of requests. Jev, though, is cheap enough that we can ask it the question thirty thousand times.

An operation that becomes a hundred or a thousand times cheaper doesn’t just make the same solution more economical, it makes possible solutions that we would previously have ruled out before even trying them.

## Thanks anyway

A few embeddings, several billion parameters, hundreds of thousands of comparisons and a reasonable amount of stubbornness later, the problem is more or less solved and Semantle can finally leave my daily life for good.

But in the end, no **daily** game will have diverted me as much as when I did everything I could to never have to play Semantle again.

So thanks, Semantle.
